What This Is

Why this code exists.

A coherent framework, not a party platform.

The Civic Code is a personal framework for evaluating government, taxation, candidates, regulation, and civic responsibility. It is not a political party platform and it is not a demand that others agree.

Its purpose is to place a connected set of ideas in one place so they can be understood as a whole rather than reduced to isolated arguments, slogans, or individual policy positions.

Readers are encouraged to challenge these ideas, adapt them, or reject them, but they should first understand the principles from which the individual rules are derived.

An Evolving Code

This framework has been developing for roughly 15–20 years. It is not intended to be frozen in time. Some principles may remain stable while individual rules are refined, narrowed, expanded, or reconsidered as new arguments, evidence, experiences, and edge cases emerge.

Changes should not be treated as inconsistency for its own sake. The goal is a more coherent framework over time. When a better formulation resolves a contradiction, clarifies an exception, or better protects the underlying principles, the code should evolve with it.

Core Mandate

Individual liberty first.

To protect individual liberty, enforce strict fiscal discipline, reject the corrupting influence of the two-party duopoly, and systematically dismantle the machinery of state expansion in favor of voluntary, consent-based community cooperation and the removal of coercive systems wherever voluntary participation is possible.

Foundations

Principles behind the rules.

The numbered rules that follow are practical applications of a smaller set of underlying principles.

Liberty & Consent

A majority should not be able to force an unwilling minority to participate in or finance an initiative merely because it received more votes. Civic cooperation should be based on consent wherever participation can be made voluntary, and coercive systems should not be preserved merely because they are traditional or politically convenient.

Personal Responsibility

Individual liberty carries obligations. People should bear responsibility for their choices, commitments, families, communities, and voluntary agreements rather than shifting every problem to the state.

Property & Ownership

Private property should be treated as genuine ownership, not as a revocable privilege conditioned on perpetual political approval. Government should face a high burden before restricting, seizing, or attaching recurring claims to what individuals own.

Subsidiarity & Local Control

Decisions should be made at the lowest practical level: individual first, then household, community, local government, state, and only then higher levels when a broader authority is genuinely necessary.

Fiscal Restraint & Transparency

Government should operate within defined means, explain the full cost and duration of what it proposes, review existing programs before expanding them, and reduce debt or burdens before inventing new uses for surplus revenue.

Equal Rules, No Favored Class

Government should not use subsidies, special tax treatment, regulation, or political access to create privileged winners and protected incumbents. Rules should be general, visible, and applied consistently.

Civic Practice Under the Current System

How the code responds to the system that exists now.

The voting rules that follow should not be mistaken for the desired end state. They describe how the principles of this code are applied defensively within the political and fiscal system that currently exists.

The current system often presents choices between major-party candidates, compulsory funding mechanisms, expanding regulatory authority, and ballot measures that bind people who did not consent. The rules in Parts I–IV are therefore practical responses to an inadequate system, not a claim that these defensive habits would remain necessary under a system built around the principles described above.

Part I

Candidate Selection & Representative Accountability

This code rejects party-line branding at all levels of government, substituting political marketing with personal knowledge, demonstrated character, or strict ideological alignment so that state power is not expanded merely for partisan advantage.

Rule 1

The Local Exception

Those who adopt this code may evaluate and vote for individual candidates at the local municipal and county levels, where government is closest to the citizen and a candidate's record, conduct, and community impact are more directly observable.

Rule 2

The Higher-Office Boycott

Those who adopt this code withhold their vote from candidates for State or Federal office as a default position, declining to participate in the major-party infrastructure and political marketing machine simply because a ballot presents a choice between competing political brands.

Rule 3

The Exception of Personal Knowledge & Trust

Condition: Rules 1 and 2 may be overridden for a candidate at any level, including State or Federal office, only when the voter personally knows the individual, respects their true intentions, and trusts their character to resist unnecessary expansion of state power.

Rule 4

The Allowance of Compromise

Under the specific exception in Rule 3, compromise on secondary policy points is acceptable when personal integrity, demonstrated trustworthiness, and a shared fundamental worldview provide a strong enough foundation for cooperation.

Rule 5

The Third-Party Outlet

A third-party candidate may earn a vote when the voter genuinely agrees with the candidate's platform. When a third-party candidate requires compromise on issues the voter considers non-negotiable, abstaining from that race remains the preferred option.

Part II

Public Finance, Taxation, and Levies

Under this code, the ballot serves as a defensive restraint against the financial expansion of the state.

Rule 6

The Fiscal Default

Those who adopt this code vote NO by default on new levies, new taxes, and extensions of existing temporary taxes. The burden of proof rests entirely on government to operate within its current means. A 51% majority should not possess the moral authority to force a 49% minority to fund an initiative against their will.

Rule 7

The Repeal Clause

Those who adopt this code vote YES on measures whose effect is to remove or allow the expiration of existing levies and taxes.

Part III

Bureaucracy & Regulations

This code treats regulation as a restriction on personal or economic liberty that must justify its continued existence rather than receive automatic deference.

Rule 8

Regulatory Containment

Those who adopt this code vote NO by default on ballot measures or initiatives that introduce new codes, compliance mandates, or regulatory authority.

Rule 9

Regulatory Deconstruction

Those who adopt this code vote YES on measures that explicitly repeal, downscale, or eliminate existing regulations, thereby expanding the sphere of individual choice.

Part IV

Property Expression & Visual Independence

This code treats private property as an extension of personal expression and rejects the use of that property as automatic advertising space for candidates, parties, or campaigns seeking political power.

Rule 10

The Campaign Sign Prohibition

Those who adopt this code do not display political campaign or candidate signage on their property. Private property should not serve as a free billboard for political brands seeking power over others.

Rule 11

The Household Accommodation Exception

A follower of this code may permit a non-candidate sign supporting a local cause, service, or community initiative out of respect for a spouse, family member, or other member of the household with a personal connection to that cause. This accommodation applies to the display of a sign only. It does not require, imply, or permit a change in the follower's vote and does not constitute abandonment of the code's fiscal principles.

Rule 12

The Generic Support Exception

Generic, non-candidate signs that express support for a community service, organization, or cause are permitted. Such expression represents voluntary human support for the people or mission involved and must not be interpreted as automatic endorsement of the taxation, levy, regulation, or funding mechanism associated with it.

Reform Principles & Proposed Frameworks

What the system should move toward.

Opposition alone is not a governing philosophy. The purpose of this code is not simply to reject existing systems, but to identify ways they could be replaced or reformed around consent, individual liberty, fiscal discipline, local accountability, and voluntary participation.

The proposals that follow vary in maturity. Some are developed frameworks. Others are principles or working ideas that remain open to revision as the Civic Code evolves.

Part V

Reforming Public Funding

The Voluntary Crowdfunded Levy Framework

Proposed System Reform · Framework Under Development

The central principle is settled: citizens who reject a publicly funded initiative should not be required to finance it merely because a majority approved it. The purpose of this framework is to replace coercive funding with voluntary participation. What remains open is how willing participants should fund an approved initiative while preserving both voluntary participation and fiscal predictability.

Rule 13

The Exemption Right

Any ballot measure or levy requiring public funding should operate on an opt-in framework. A “No” vote constitutes an exemption from that specific initiative. Only those voluntarily supporting the measure may be assessed for its cost.

Rule 14

The Universal Access Principle

No person may be denied access to a publicly funded service, facility, program, or community benefit solely because they voted “No” or declined to participate financially. Once an initiative meets its required threshold and takes effect, ordinary eligibility and access rules apply equally without regard to how a person voted or whether they helped fund it.

This framework intentionally accepts the possibility that some people will benefit without contributing financially. Access to a public benefit should not be conditioned on how a person voted or whether that person chose to help fund it.

Funding Design Under Development

How should participating voters fund an approved initiative?

Two funding models remain under consideration. Both preserve the exemption right in Rule 13 and the universal access principle in Rule 14, but they distribute financial responsibility differently.

Open Question

The Civic Code does not yet choose between these models. Any final system should balance voluntary participation, predictable individual liability, meaningful project funding, and protection against strategic voting incentives. Under either model, access remains governed by Rule 14 rather than by a person's funding decision.

Rule 15

The Crowdfunding Threshold Mandate

Every voluntarily funded initiative must establish a legally binding minimum participation threshold before the vote. If participation falls below that threshold, the initiative does not proceed and no participant is charged.

The threshold serves two purposes. Under Option A, it prevents a small number of Yes voters from being saddled with an unreasonable share of the total cost. Under Option B, it prevents an initiative from proceeding with funding so low that its stated purpose cannot realistically be achieved.

Part VI

Residence-Based Municipal Taxation

Proposed Ohio Constitutional Reform

Municipal government should derive personal income-tax authority from residency, not merely from the location of a person's employer.

Rule 16

The Resident Taxation Principle

A municipality should not impose an income tax on an individual solely because that person works, performs services, or earns wages within the municipality while residing somewhere else. Personal municipal income taxation should follow residency.

Rule 17

The Representation Principle

A nonresident worker does not vote for that municipality's elected officials and does not participate directly in decisions about its tax rates, spending priorities, or local government. A municipality should not finance itself by imposing personal income taxes on people who have no electoral voice in that government.

Rule 18

The Constitutional Safeguard

This principle should be protected by the Ohio Constitution rather than left to ordinary municipal policy. The amendment should prohibit a municipal corporation from taxing the compensation of an individual who is not a resident of that municipality solely because the individual performs employment or personal services there.

Current Ohio Context

Ohio law currently permits municipal income taxation of qualifying income earned by nonresidents within a municipality. This proposal would change that constitutional baseline for individual workers. It is aimed specifically at personal municipal income tax on nonresident labor, not at defining the taxation of businesses or every possible fee for a specific service. Ohio Revised Code Chapter 718

Developing

Ideas Still Being Formulated

These ideas belong in the Civic Code because they follow from its underlying principles, but they are not yet settled enough to become firm rules. They are recorded here so the direction of thought is visible without pretending the details are finished.

Federal Taxation

Simplify or replace the federal income-tax system.

The current direction of thought favors a federal tax system that is simpler, more predictable, and less intrusive, while protecting a meaningful level of basic income from federal income taxation and pairing tax reform with strict spending restraint.

Models considered over time have included a 15% flat federal income tax above an income floor, with payroll taxes treated separately, as well as a FairTax-style national consumption tax with a prebate designed to offset taxation on basic necessities. No final choice between those approaches is established here.

Any tax reform should be paired with aggressive control of federal spending and meaningful debt reduction rather than treating a new tax structure as permission to preserve or expand existing spending automatically.

Status of Developing Ideas

Not doctrine. Not promises.

Ideas in this section may be refined, combined, rejected, or replaced. Their inclusion records a direction of thought, not a final commitment. When an idea becomes coherent enough to establish a durable principle or rule, it can move into the formal Civic Code.
